# The Terms: Don & Doff

"Donning" and "doffing" are real spaceflight terms — not brand invention.
They refer to the two bookends of every mission: suiting up, and suiting
down.

**Origin.** The words themselves predate spaceflight — originally technical
terms used for legal purposes in the labor relations field. They crossed
into the space community's working vocabulary decades later, as NASA's
human spaceflight program grew and suiting procedures needed precise,
unambiguous language.

**Gemini (1966).** By the Gemini program, the terms were already standard
in NASA engineering documentation. NASA Technical Note TN D-3291 (1966)
describes the Gemini suit design approach as one followed to simplify
donning and doffing. Gemini spacesuit gloves were designed so
an astronaut could don and doff them without assistance; the suit's torso
connections were engineered specifically to simplify donning and doffing.

**Apollo.** The procedure became drilled routine. Astronauts rehearsed it
over and over — because in an emergency, they might have as little as five
minutes to get fully suited, compared to a much longer, careful process
during normal mission prep.

**Modern era.** The vocabulary never went away. NASA's Commercial Crew
program marked the 2020 Demo-2 launch — the first crewed liftoff from U.S.
soil since the Shuttle retired — with a post headlined "Bob and Doug 'Go'
for Suit Donning." Engineers have since designed hardware (donning/doffing
stands, "suitport" systems) purpose-built to make the process faster and
safer.

**Still the standard.** Today, NASA's human spaceflight standards
(NASA-STD-3001) include a formal requirement, V2 11001, that spacesuits
accommodate efficient and effective donning and doffing in both nominal and
contingency operations.

**Why the name.** Every mission starts the same way: you suit up. Every
mission ends the same way: you suit down. Don & Doff Co. takes its name from
that complete cycle — the leaving, and the coming home.
